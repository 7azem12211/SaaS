Overview

Tidyline is a fictional product I invented for my portfolio. There are no real users or analytics behind it, so this case study is about the decisions I made, not measured results.

Tidyline is an imagined task tool for small teams. The brief I gave myself was simple: build the landing page I would want to land on, one that tells me what the product is without making me read a feature list.

Many SaaS pages bury the product under buzzwords. I wanted the opposite: a short promise, something to click right away, and a clear next step.

Goals

I wrote four goals before drawing anything, and checked every later decision against them.

Clear in 5 secondsOne headline and one sentence say what Tidyline does.
Show, don't tellA working task board lets visitors feel the product at once.
Easy pricingTwo plans, plain language, and a billing switch.
Works for everyoneResponsive, keyboard friendly, and calm for people who prefer less motion.

Assumed audience: team leads in groups of 5 to 20 who are tired of tools with too many features. This is an assumption, not research.

Process

  1. Words first. I wrote the headline, the supporting sentence, and three benefits before opening a design tool. If the message is weak, no layout can save it.
  2. Order the page by visitor questions. What is it? How does it help? Can I see it? How much? What if I have doubts? That gave me the hero, features, numbers, pricing, FAQ, and a final call to action.
  3. Pick a small visual system. One typeface, one action color, one dark color, one soft success color. Fewer choices made the page feel more confident.
  4. Build in plain code. No frameworks or libraries, so the page loads fast and every line is mine to explain.
  5. Add depth. After the basics worked, I layered in interaction and subtle 3D, then tested how much was too much.

Build and accessibility

The page is one dependency-free file, built with:

HTML5CSS custom propertiesCSS GridVanilla JavaScriptIntersectionObserver
  • Responsive: the layout collapses to a single column on small screens, and the 3D tilt is switched off there.
  • Semantic controls: the billing switch uses a labeled button with aria-pressed, tabs report their selected state, and the FAQ uses native details elements.
  • Visible focus: every interactive element shows a clear focus ring.
  • Motion: transitions are removed for people who prefer reduced motion.

What I would do next

A concept is never finished. These are the honest gaps I would close first:

  • Test the page with five real people and watch where they hesitate.
  • Measure performance and accessibility with proper tools instead of assuming.
  • Add arrow-key navigation to the tabs, which currently work by click and Tab key only.
  • Turn off the pointer-driven tilt completely when reduced motion is on, not just the transitions.
  • Replace the placeholder mockups with real product screens.
